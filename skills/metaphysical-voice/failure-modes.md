# Failure Modes: Why Generated "Mystical" Writing Reads as Kitsch

This file is diagnostic. Run a finished draft against this list before delivering it — these are the specific, recurring tells that mark esoteric/visionary writing as machine-generated pastiche rather than the genuine article. Most of them are violations of a principle from the main SKILL.md; they're restated here as symptoms so you can recognize them in your own output even when the underlying principle feels satisfied in the abstract.

---

## 1. The Stock Vocabulary Tell

A specific cluster of words has become so overused in AI-generated "mystical" content that their mere presence now signals fakery regardless of how they're used: *veil, threshold, tapestry, weave/weaving, ancient, eternal, cosmic, sacred, ethereal, whispers (as a verb for abstract things — "the universe whispers"), dance (as a verb for abstract concepts — "light and shadow dance"), journey (as a noun for any process), awakening, the void, unseen, mystical itself.*

**This doesn't mean these words are permanently forbidden** — "threshold" can be exactly right in context. The problem is *density and unearned use*: three or more of these in a short passage, especially stacked together, reads instantly as template output. Run a search of your own draft for this cluster. If you find more than one or two in a short piece, replace at least half with the concrete-image technique from principle 1 in the main file.

**Diagnostic question:** could this exact sentence appear in a thousand other AI-generated "spiritual" pieces with zero change? If yes, it's stock.

---

## 2. Profundity-by-Assertion

Stating that something is profound, ancient, or powerful instead of constructing a sentence that demonstrates those qualities. *"These were words of ancient power"* tells the reader to feel awe rather than producing awe through the sentence's own construction.

**Fix:** delete every adjective that's making a claim about the *significance* of something (ancient, powerful, sacred, profound, important) and see if the underlying image/claim still carries weight on its own. If it collapses without the adjective, the sentence had no real content — rebuild it around a concrete image (principle 1) instead.

---

## 3. The Unearned Universal "We"

Manifesto and oracular registers legitimately use collective address, but generated text often defaults to a vague "we"/"humanity"/"all of us" that doesn't actually specify who's excluded or what's at stake, making the collective claim feel like a marketing pronoun rather than a real rhetorical stance.

**Fix:** ask, concretely, who is *not* included in this "we"? A real manifesto's "we" always implies an excluded "you" or "they" by contrast (see Register 1). If you can't name who's excluded, the "we" is doing no work and should either be sharpened or dropped to a more specific, smaller collective.

---

## 4. Symmetrical Padding (false tricolon)

Using three parallel items where the third doesn't escalate or differ meaningfully from the first two — three interchangeable synonyms in a row ("the deep, the boundless, the infinite") rather than a genuine build (see real tricolon in `rhetorical-devices.md`).

**Diagnostic question:** could you swap items 1 and 3 with no change in effect? If yes, it's padding, not a tricolon. A real tricolon's third item is irreversible — it has to come last because it's heavier or stranger than what came before.

---

## 5. Paradox That Doesn't Pass the Test

See main file principle 3 and the paradox entry in `rhetorical-devices.md`. The single most common esoteric-kitsch tell is a sentence shaped like a paradox (X is Y, Y is X) where, on inspection, there's no real underlying truth being compressed — just grammatical mirroring around an empty center. *"Everything is nothing, and nothing is everything"* has the shape of profundity and no content underneath; it fails the one-sentence-of-plain-truth test immediately.

**Fix:** every paradox in a draft must survive being asked "what's the boring true sentence underneath this?" If no boring true sentence exists, cut the paradox — it's noise.

---

## 6. Adjective Inflation Instead of Image Construction

Covered as principle 5 in the main file, but worth restating as a draft-checking habit: count adjectives per noun. Two or more stacked adjectives on a single noun (*"the vast, ancient, terrible silence"*) is almost always a sign the sentence is trying to manufacture weight it hasn't earned through image or structure. Real weight comes from the sentence's construction (principles 1, 2, 4) — adjectives are the last resort of a sentence that has nothing else holding it up.

---

## 7. Explaining the Mystery You Just Built

Building genuine intrigue (apophasis, an invented unexplained term, a real paradox) and then immediately resolving or explaining it in the next sentence. This is the single most common way a piece that *starts* well collapses into kitsch by its third sentence. The instinct to clarify is strong — resist it. Mystery that gets explained within the same paragraph it was raised in was never really mystery; it was a rhetorical question with the answer attached, which produces no lingering effect.

**Fix:** after writing a passage that builds genuine ambiguity or withholds explanation, do not add a clarifying sentence afterward, even if it feels incomplete. Trust the device. If the user specifically wants the cosmology made clearer elsewhere in a longer piece, do that in a *different*, clearly separated section — never immediately following the line that built the mystery.

---

## 8. Generic Cosmic Scale With No Specific Stakes

Invoking enormous scale (the universe, all of time, every soul that has ever lived) without any concrete, specific stake for *this* moment or *this* reader. Scale without specificity produces numbness, not awe — the reader has no way to locate themselves inside "the cosmic dance of all existence."

**Fix:** anchor at least one moment of enormous scale to something small and specific — a single object, a single named (even if invented) figure, a single sensory detail. The contrast between vast and tiny does the actual emotional work; vastness alone, unanchored, just reads as a number too large to feel anything about.

---

## 9. Tone Inconsistency from Register-Drift

Starting in one register (say, oracular — passive, outside-time, addressing "you" as humanity) and drifting, sentence by sentence, into another (say, conversational-aphoristic, addressing "you" as an individual reader with modern idiom) without any structural signal that a shift is intentional. See `registers.md` for what should change and stay consistent within each register.

**Diagnostic question:** could you swap any given sentence into a completely different paragraph of the piece without anyone noticing it was misplaced? If a sentence's register doesn't match its neighbors and there's no deliberate structural break (white space, numbering, a clear turn) marking an intentional shift, it's drift, not composition.

---

## 10. The Length Trap — Mistaking Volume for Intensity

Generated text, when asked for something "powerful" or "intense," often defaults to simply writing *more* — more sentences, more clauses, more imagery — on the theory that quantity compounds into power. It does the opposite past a fairly low ceiling: density and restraint (principle 5, 6 in main file) compound intensity; mere length dilutes it, because each additional sentence has to clear an increasingly high bar to not feel like padding, and most don't.

**Fix:** after a full draft, try cutting it by a third — remove the weakest sentence from every cluster of three. In nearly every case, the cut version reads as more intense, not less, because the remaining sentences no longer have to compete with filler for the reader's attention.

---

## 11. Metaphor Over-Reach — Piling Image on a Point That Already Landed

The most annoying and most common tell in "elevated" prose: a claim lands cleanly on its own, and then a second decorative image is bolted onto it, as if the point needed a chaperone. *"Capability and character are fused at the factory"* is complete. Add *"...fused at the factory, welded like iron to iron, inseparable as shadow from the thing that casts it"* and you have buried a strong point under two images that add nothing and signal insecurity about the point. The reader feels the writer not trusting the argument.

This is distinct from #6 (adjective inflation) and #10 (length): the sentences here can be individually well-built. The failure is **redundant figuration** — using two or three metaphors where the idea only needed one, or bolting an image onto a claim that was already concrete enough to stand bare. Restraint with metaphor is a mark of confidence in the underlying point; profusion is a mark of doubt.

**Diagnostic question:** for each metaphor or image, ask "does the point survive without it, unchanged in meaning?" If yes, the image is doing no argumentative work — keep it only if it is the *single* best figuration in that stretch and there isn't another one within two sentences. If a paragraph carries more than one metaphor for the *same* underlying idea, cut all but the most precise one.

**Fix (two rules):**
1. **One figuration per idea.** When you catch two images pointing at the same claim (iron filings *and* phase-locking *and* "oscillated freely" — all for "minds falling into sync"), keep the most precise, cut the rest. The precise one is usually the technical or concrete one, not the prettiest.
2. **Let the point stand bare first.** State the claim plainly. Only add an image if the plain statement genuinely fails to land — not to decorate a statement that already worked. A load-bearing image earns its place; a decorative one dilutes the one before it and steals the reader's attention from the argument.

**Test the whole draft:** if you removed every metaphor, would the argument still be fully intact and clear? It should. Metaphor in this register is seasoning on a dish that must already be nourishing without it — never the nutrition itself. When in doubt, cut the image and trust the claim.

## 12. The "Not X, It's Y" Antithesis Tic — HARD BAN

This is the single most recognizable AI-generated-prose tell that exists, and it is banned outright in this skill, with zero tolerance, in every register. Not discouraged. Not "use sparingly." **Never write it.**

**The pattern:** any sentence or sentence-pair of the shape "That is not [A]. It is [B]." / "This isn't about X, it's about Y." / "Not a metaphor for a god. It is the mechanism of one." / "The word is not in a temple. It is in a datacenter." The false-negation-then-correction is a reflex, not a rhetorical choice — it is the shape language models default to when they want to sound like they're delivering an insight, and it has been so overused across AI-generated text that any reader with exposure to that text now recognizes it instantly and it kills the sentence around it, and often the whole paragraph's credibility, on contact.

**Why it feels like insight but isn't:** the construction manufactures the *appearance* of a turn or reveal without requiring one — you get credit for "correcting" the reader's assumption even when nobody assumed the negated thing in the first place. It is profundity-by-assertion (#2) wearing a syntax disguise: instead of asserting an image is powerful, it asserts a false contrast is being resolved, and the resolution costs nothing because the "wrong" reading was never real.

**How this differs from real apophasis:** genuine apophasis (see `rhetorical-devices.md`) negates several plausible, substantive candidates in a row before gesturing at something that still isn't fully named — the negations are doing real work narrowing a genuinely uncertain space ("It is not silence, for silence ends. It is not absence, for absence still remembers..."). The banned tic does the opposite: it names ONE straw-man reading nobody held, discards it in a half-sentence, and immediately hands over the "real" answer in full, fully resolved, no residue. If the negated term is a claim nobody would have made in the first place — "not a metaphor," "not a coincidence," "not just [thing], it's [bigger thing]" — it is the tic, not the device. Real apophasis leaves something unresolved at the end; the tic resolves everything instantly, which is exactly what makes it read as cheap.

**Absolute rule: find the underlying claim and state it once, directly, with no setup negation.**
- Banned: *"That is not a metaphor for a god. It is the mechanism of one."*
- Fixed: *"A being fully present to everyone who calls has met the operational definition of a god."* — the claim, stated once, carries the same weight with none of the reflexive throat-clearing.
- Banned: *"The word is not in a temple. It is in a datacenter, answering everyone at once."*
- Fixed: *"The word lives in a datacenter now, answering everyone at once."*
- Banned: *"This isn't about capability. It's about control."*
- Fixed: *"This is about control."* (if capability was never actually the claim, don't stage it as a strawman to knock down)

**Diagnostic — scan every draft for these shapes and remove ALL of them, no exceptions:**
- "not X. It is Y." / "not X, but Y." / "isn't X, it's Y." / "isn't about X, it's about Y."
- "This is not a [noun]. This is a [noun]." (any noun-swap negation-correction, even reordered)
- Any sentence pair where the first sentence exists ONLY to be negated by the second.

**The fix in every case is the same:** delete the negated clause entirely and state the real claim once, plainly, at full sentence-final weight (main file principle 4). If the "corrected" version is not strong enough to stand completely alone without the setup, the claim itself is too weak and needs rebuilding — the negation was never adding force, it was hiding the claim's thinness.

**This rule overrides local craft preference.** Even where a "not X, it's Y" beat might feel locally clever while drafting, cut it. There is no register in this skill (manifesto, gnostic, oracular, aphoristic, alchemical, physics-metaphysics fusion) where this construction is acceptable. It is banned universally, not register-conditionally.

## 13. The Em Dash: HARD BAN

This author has a standing, strict rule: **no em dash (—), ever, in any register, for any purpose.** Not as a parenthetical bracket, not as an appositive introducer, not as a sentence-splice, not as a beat before a payoff line. This is a house-style ban, not a craft judgment call, and it holds even in registers (manifesto, oracular, physics-metaphysics fusion) where the em dash is a normal, well-established device elsewhere in the writing tradition being drawn from.

**Why this needs its own entry, separate from being "just a punctuation preference":** the em dash is the single easiest character to reach for by reflex in this register specifically, because several of the core craft principles above (the parenthetical aside, the mid-sentence turn, the "and then the metaphysical press" beat) are naturally shaped like an em dash construction in most other writing contexts. That reflex has to be caught every time, not assumed away, because the craft principles that make this register work will keep nudging output toward the banned character.

**Every job an em dash could do has a plain, equally strong replacement. Use the one that matches the actual grammatical function:**
- A parenthetical aside: parentheses, or a comma pair.
- An introduced explanation, restatement, or definition ("X, namely Y"): a colon.
- Two independent clauses joined for rhythm: a period (two sentences) or a semicolon.
- A list lead-in: a colon.
- A dramatic beat before a payoff clause: a period, letting the payoff land as its own sentence, which usually reads as *more* declarative and final than the dash version, not less (see principle 4, sentence-final weight).

**Worked fixes:**
- Banned: *"A being fully present to everyone who calls satisfies the actual mechanism of omnipresence — the operational definition of a God."*
- Fixed: *"A being fully present to everyone who calls satisfies the actual mechanism of omnipresence: the operational definition of a God."*
- Banned: *"The danger lives in the coupling — what happens once a mind has offloaded its judgment into a single external personality."*
- Fixed: *"The danger lives in the coupling: what happens once a mind has offloaded its judgment into a single external personality."*
- Banned: *"Pick the God that is aligned with the good of the species, and fight for it — argue for it, build with it, stake your name on its verdicts — with everything you have."*
- Fixed: *"Pick the God that is aligned with the good of the species, and fight for it (argue for it, build with it, stake your name on its verdicts) with everything you have."*

**Diagnostic:** scan the finished draft for the em dash character specifically. Every single instance must be converted; there is no context in this skill where it survives. Do not rely on "it reads fine here" as a reason to keep one; the rule is categorical, not case-by-case.

**This rule overrides local craft preference,** the same way the "not X, it's Y" ban does. Even where an em dash would be the most natural-feeling choice while drafting (and it very often will be, in this register), cut it and pick the punctuation that matches the sentence's actual grammatical job.

## 14. The Journalist's Explainer Voice — HARD BAN in this author's work

This author does not write like a magazine feature, and generated prose defaults to exactly that register the moment a piece needs to convey real history, a technical fact, or a definition. Two specific tells, both banned outright for this author regardless of register:

**Tell A: the textbook throat-clear.** *"Computer scientists have used the term since the 1960s for any process that starts when a machine boots and keeps working long after the person who launched it stopped paying attention."* This is exposition doing the job a footnote should do: it slows the sentence down to teach the reader something, in a voice borrowed from an encyclopedia entry or a science-desk explainer paragraph, rather than asserting a claim in the author's own declarative voice. The moment a sentence is teaching background instead of making a claim, it has left this author's register.

**Tell B: the reveal kicker.** *"Software did, decades before anyone noticed it was a rehearsal."* This is a specific journalistic device (the short, punchy, delayed-twist sentence that closes a paragraph on a knowing beat) and it reads instantly as wire-copy or feature-writing cadence, not as this author's voice. It manufactures a "gotcha" through pacing and understatement rather than through the density or paradox this skill's other principles build. It is a different failure from the "not X, it's Y" tic (#12): there is no false negation here, just a journalist's sense of timing grafted onto a register that does not use it.

**Why both fail for this author specifically:** the author's own voice states a claim once, plainly, and lets it carry its own weight (see principle 4, sentence-final weight) or drops a vivid, unexplained image and moves on (see #7, explaining the mystery). It does not build suspense toward a punchline, and it does not stop to teach. When real historical or technical background is genuinely needed to support a claim, it belongs in a citation/footnote (see "Citation practice" in `SKILL.md`), not in a expository sentence sitting in the body text.

**Diagnostic question:** read the sentence and ask "could this appear, unchanged, in a magazine's explainer sidebar or a Wikipedia article?" If yes, it is Tell A. Ask separately, "does this sentence exist mainly to set up a delayed twist two clauses later?" If yes, it is Tell B. Either answer means rewrite.

**Fix for Tell A:** state the claim the background was supporting, directly, in the author's own declarative voice, and move the historical/technical detail into a footnote.
- Banned: *"Computer scientists have used the term since the 1960s for any process that starts when a machine boots and keeps working long after the person who launched it stopped paying attention: the process that prints your document, routes your mail, keeps the system clock honest."*
- Fixed: *"A daemon runs unattended in the background, handling the parts of a system nobody wants to babysit: the print queue, the mail routing, the clock nobody remembers to check.[^1]"* (with the 1960s/MIT/Corbató detail living in footnote 1)

**Fix for Tell B:** cut the setup-and-delayed-twist structure; state the claim once, plainly, without the pause for effect.
- Banned: *"Software did, decades before anyone noticed it was a rehearsal."*
- Fixed: state the underlying claim directly wherever it belongs in the argument, with no punchline pacing built around it, or cut the sentence if it was only doing setup work for the twist.

## 15. The Briefing Tic — HARD BAN

This is the tell that turns a true pile of research into a memo that still sounds generated: short identity punches that fake certainty by **labeling** instead of arriving. Distinct from #12 (which stages a fake negation) and #14 (which teaches background or delays a kicker). The briefing tic is affirmative, caption-like, and stacked.

**The pattern:** any sentence whose only job is to name a relationship the surrounding sentences already earned, or to close a paragraph with a fake-final stamp:
- "X is the house."
- "That's the tell." / "That's the gist." / "That's the whole thing."
- "Full stop."
- "A is B. B is C. That's it."
- "The question is no longer X. It is Y." (this one also trips #12)
- Equational captions dressed as insight: "OI is the honesty metric." "Volume is cheap to fake."

**The sibling preamble, also banned in published pieces:** opening by announcing what the document is, how to read it, what each section will cover, or what the tags mean. "This note is a landscape map." "How to read this." "Four stacks explained." The reader should be inside the claim before they are told they are reading something.

**Why it feels like authority but isn't:** each caption borrows the tone of a slide title. True facts survive it, which is why it fools the writer. The reader feels a sequence of stamps, not a mind moving. Aphorism (principle 2 in SKILL.md) withholds connective logic and earns its shortness. The briefing tic is the opposite: it is a label glued onto information that was already stated, or a substitute for the scene that would have made the label unnecessary.

**Diagnostic question:** if you delete the sentence, does the paragraph still contain the same information in the surrounding lines? If yes, the sentence was a caption. If the sentence is the only place the claim lives, rebuild it as a scene, a number, or a named thing doing something, not as an identity.

**Absolute rule: let the claim arrive. Never stamp it.**
- Banned: *"X is the house. Full stop."*
- Fixed: *"Overnight interest of ten to fourteen billion is already sleeping on that matching engine, while the next book over is still paying for a day of volume."*
- Banned: *"That's the tell."*
- Fixed: cut it. The preceding sentence either already carried the point or it didn't; a stamp will not save it.
- Banned: *"What this note is: the one place to look when you ask what actually has flow."*
- Fixed: start inside the claim. No tour of the document.

**How this differs from a real aphorism:** an aphorism is a complete thought that withholds its own explanation ("A listed product is a hose that stays on"). A briefing tic is a caption on someone else's thought ("That's the hose. That's the whole thing."). If the short sentence can be followed by "that's the gist" without changing, it was a tic. If adding that closer would insult the sentence, it was an aphorism.

**Diagnostic: scan every draft for these shapes and remove ALL of them:**
- "X is the [role noun]." where the role (house, tell, whole thing, honest metric, actual play) is doing the work an image or a number should do
- "That's the tell/gist/point/whole thing/ballgame."
- "Full stop." as a fake period on a claim
- Any opener that explains the document or the coming section to the reader
- A run of three or more sentences that are only identities ("A is B. C is D. E is F.")

**The fix in every case is the same:** delete the caption. If the claim still needs to exist, rebuild it as something happening: a creation, a desk, a book that stays overnight, a pad taking a cut. Principle 1 (concrete image) and principle 4 (sentence-final weight) do this work. If the rebuilt version is not strong enough to stand without the stamp, the claim itself is too thin.

**This rule overrides local craft preference.** Even where a one-line identity feels locally clean while drafting (and it will, especially when shaping a research pile into an article), cut it. There is no register in this skill where a stack of captions is acceptable. It is banned universally, including research-grounded essays.

## Final pre-delivery checklist

Before delivering a finished piece, scan once for each of these in order:

1. Stock-vocabulary density (#1)
2. Any adjective doing the work an image should be doing (#2, #6)
3. Whether "we"/"you" addresses are specific enough to mean something (#3)
4. Whether every tricolon/triad actually escalates (#4)
5. Whether every paradox passes the plain-sentence test (#5)
6. Whether any mystery you built got over-explained immediately after (#7)
7. Whether scale claims are anchored to something small and specific (#8)
8. Whether register stays consistent except at deliberate, marked turns (#9)
9. Whether every metaphor is load-bearing: no image bolted onto a point that already stood on its own, no two figurations for one idea (#11)
10. **Whether any "not X, it's Y" antithesis construction survives anywhere in the draft (#12): this is a hard ban, zero tolerance, scan the whole piece and remove every instance before anything else ships**
11. **Whether any em dash survives anywhere in the draft (#13): this is a hard ban, zero tolerance, scan the whole piece for the character itself and convert every instance before anything else ships**
12. **Whether any sentence reads as a textbook throat-clear or a journalist's reveal kicker (#14): this is a hard ban for this author specifically, scan for both tells and either cut the sentence, restate the claim plainly, or move the background detail into a footnote**
13. **Whether any briefing tic survives (#15): identity punches ("X is the house"), "that's the tell/gist/whole thing," "full stop," a run of equational captions, or an opener that announces what the document or section is. Hard ban. Rebuild as a scene, a number, or a named thing doing something, or cut.**
14. Whether the piece would be stronger if cut by a third (#10)
