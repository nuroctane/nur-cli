# Registers: Distinguishing the Voices

Each of these is a genuinely different grammatical engine, not a different vocabulary list painted over the same sentence shape. The fastest way to make generated "mystical" writing feel undifferentiated is to use the same subject-verb-object declarative sentence for all of them and just swap in fancier nouns. Don't do that. Below: what actually changes at the level of grammar, pronoun, verb mood, and address for each register.

---

## 1. The Manifesto

**Source of authority:** collective will, historical necessity, the future tense treated as already certain.

**Grammar:**
- Heavy use of **first-person plural** ("we"), rarely singular. The speaker is a movement, not a person.
- **Present-tense declaratives stated as facts about the future**: not "we will build" but "we build" — manifestos collapse the future into an eternal present to make the new order sound already real.
- **Negation-then-affirmation pairing**: state what is rejected, then immediately what replaces it, often in matched syntax ("No more X. Only Y.").
- Imperative mood addressed outward, to an implied opposition or to history itself, not gently to "you the reader."
- Numbered or list-based structure is native to this register — manifestos historically come as numbered points (see Futurist, Surrealist, Dada manifesto traditions) because the numbering itself performs systematization and confidence.

**Address to reader:** The reader is either recruited ("join us") or cast as the old order being superseded ("you who still believe..."). Rarely neutral.

**Example shape** (original, not quoted from any source):
> We have no patience left for the careful. The careful built nothing that did not first betray its own foundations. We declare: the sentence is a weapon or it is furniture. We will not furnish anything again.

**Common failure:** manifesto voice softened with hedges ("we believe," "we feel that," "in our view"). A manifesto never believes — it knows, or performs knowing. Strip every hedge word; if the user wants nuance, that nuance goes in surrounding prose outside the manifesto-voice passages, not inside them.

## 2. The Gnostic / Hermetic Fragment

**Source of authority:** concealed knowledge now partially revealed; the speaker has access the reader does not, and the text's job is to gesture at it without fully transmitting it.

**Grammar:**
- **Passive and impersonal constructions dominate**: "it is given," "that which is hidden," "it was shown" — agency is deliberately obscured. Compare to manifesto's aggressive active-voice "we."
- **Relative clauses stacked as titles/epithets** rather than plain nouns: not "God" but "that which has no name and is called by a thousand," not "the soul" but "the spark that remembers a fire it has never seen."
- **Apophasis** (saying through unsaying) is the signature device here — see `rhetorical-devices.md`. Definitions proceed by negation: "It is not light, for light ends. It is not dark, for dark is only light's absence, and this absence has no opposite."
- Frequent use of **correlative pairs presented as a key/cipher** ("as above, so below"; "what binds also unbinds") — a single relation, stated once, that the rest of the text treats as a law everything else follows from.

**Address to reader:** Often *no direct address at all* — the reader is overhearing instruction meant for an initiate, which paradoxically pulls the reader in more than direct address would, because eavesdropping implies the content is real and unperformed.

**Example shape:**
> What was scattered does not remember being whole, and so it calls its scattering freedom. The one who gathers it back will be called a thief by everything it gathers.

**Common failure:** over-explaining the cipher. If you state "as above so below" and then spend three sentences explaining what that means literally, you've killed the device — gnostic texts trust the reader to sit with partial understanding. Give the law once, apply it without re-explaining it, and move on.

## 3. The Oracular / Prophetic Voice

**Source of authority:** speaking from outside time; the speaker is a vessel or conduit, not an author.

**Grammar:**
- **Future tense used as inevitability**, often paired with present-tense scene-setting: "A man will come who has already arrived." This temporal folding — future stated as already happening — is the single most identifiable oracular device.
- **Second person used at a distance**: "you" as humankind or a nation, not an individual reader — closer to apostrophe (addressing an abstraction as if it could hear) than to direct conversation.
- **Parataxis** (short independent clauses joined with "and," minimal subordination) mimicking translated-scripture cadence: "And the gate opened. And no one was glad." This is a deliberate, learnable rhythm, not randomness — see King James Bible cadence as the most famous English example of the *pattern* (not to be quoted, but the structural rhythm — short parallel clauses, "and" as connective tissue — is freely usable, since it's a grammatical pattern, not protected text).
- Numbers and time-markers used as ritual weight rather than information: "after the third silence," "in the year the rivers forgot their names" — specific-sounding but functionally symbolic, not calendrical.

**Address to reader:** The reader is a witness to something already decided, not a participant who can change it. This produces dread or awe rather than the manifesto's recruitment-energy.

**Common failure:** oracular voice that hedges with "perhaps" or "it may be." Prophecy doesn't hedge — uncertainty, if wanted, should be structural (a prophecy that contradicts itself, or arrives in fragments) rather than expressed through hedge-words, which instantly collapse the authority of the register.

## 4. The Aphoristic-Philosophical Voice

**Source of authority:** solitary insight, often adversarial toward conventional morality or comfort; the speaker is a single mind, sharply individuated, frequently combative.

**Grammar:**
- **Short, self-contained sentences that could each stand alone**, usually not narratively connected to what comes before or after. Each aphorism is its own unit of thought, often numbered or set apart by white space.
- **Reversal structure**: state the conventional view, then invert it in the same sentence or the next. "What you call your conscience is only your fear, dressed for company." The inversion is the entire content — there's rarely elaboration after it.
- **Direct address to "you" used confrontationally**, frequently second-guessing the reader's motives rather than instructing them.
- Heavy use of irony and a willingness to be unfair to the position being attacked — this voice is not balanced, and trying to make it fair-minded destroys its energy.

**Address to reader:** Adversarial-intimate — speaks as though it already knows something shameful about the reader and is naming it without cruelty but also without softening.

**Example shape:**
> Most people do not want truth. They want a truth that does not require them to change anything else they already believe. Call this comfort, if you like the word. I call it the slow kind of cowardice — the kind that never has to look at itself, because it never stops moving.

**Common failure:** aphorisms that are just inverted clichés with no real reversal logic underneath ("the strongest are the weakest" said without the structural surprise that makes the inversion land). Test every aphorism with the paradox-test in the main SKILL.md: can you state the non-contradictory truth underneath it in one plain sentence? If not, it's noise dressed as insight.

## 5. The Alchemical / Grimoire Voice

**Source of authority:** technical-ritual precision; this voice borrows the *form* of instruction (recipe, formula, procedure) and repurposes it for symbolic/poetic content, producing a specific uncanny effect — the reader feels they are reading actual instructions for something impossible.

**Grammar:**
- **Imperative procedural structure**: "Take the [object]. Let it [process] until [condition]. What remains is [transformation]." This is recipe-grammar — directly borrowed from cookbook/lab-manual syntax — applied to metaphysical material.
- **Material specificity even when the materials are invented or symbolic**: "three drops of the hour before dawn," "the ash of a name spoken once and never again." Concreteness (principle 1 in the main file) is *most* important here, because the entire effect depends on instructions sounding executable.
- **Conditional/temporal sequencing markers** ("until," "when," "at the moment that") structure the whole passage, mimicking lab notation or recipe timing.
- Often closes with a stated transformation or yield, as a real recipe would: "And what you have made cannot be unmade, only forgotten — which is its own kind of making."

**Address to reader:** Direct second-person instruction, as if the reader is actually meant to perform the process — even though (per the scope note in the main SKILL.md) this is craft/atmosphere, not real ritual instruction.

**Common failure:** drifting out of procedural grammar into ordinary descriptive prose halfway through. Once you commit to recipe-form, sustain the imperative-conditional structure for the whole passage — switching to "and then beautifully, the soul becomes free" breaks the uncanny-literalism that makes this register work.

## Mixing registers within one piece

Sophisticated pieces often shift registers deliberately — open oracular, narrow into aphoristic, close with a manifesto-style declaration. This works when the shift is a structural choice (e.g., marking a new numbered section) rather than an accident of drift. When mixing, signal the shift with a clear structural break (white space, a numeral, a short transitional aphorism) so the register change reads as composition, not inconsistency.
