# Lineages: Borrowing Structure, Never Text

This file describes the *rhetorical mechanics* of major historical traditions that inform metaphysical/manifesto/esoteric writing — so you can write in a voice informed by a tradition's structural logic without imitating, quoting, or closely paraphrasing any specific copyrighted text. Everything below describes patterns and techniques (which are not copyrightable) rather than reproducing or closely mirroring any actual passage from any actual book.

If a user names a specific living or recently-deceased author and asks to write "in their style," that's generally fine as a style/register request (style itself isn't protected), but never attribute invented lines to that real person as if they wrote them, and never reproduce or closely paraphrase their actual text. If a user wants something from a specific religious tradition's actual scripture, point them to that scripture directly rather than generating a substitute that might be mistaken for or misrepresent the real text.

---

## The Manifesto Tradition (Futurism, Surrealism, Dada, political manifestos generally)

**Historical mechanics, generalized:** These movements developed manifesto-writing as a genre with a recognizable formal logic: numbered or list-based assertions; declarative present tense; aggressive rejection of a named predecessor or establishment; a totalizing claim to represent the future or the only valid way forward; frequent use of exclamation and direct command; willingness to be deliberately one-sided, unfair, and overstated as a rhetorical strategy rather than a flaw.

**What to borrow:** the *structural confidence* — stating contested claims as settled fact, organizing by numbered points, using rejection-then-affirmation pairing (see Register 1 in `registers.md`), and the genre's permission to be polemical rather than balanced.

**What never to do:** attribute an invented manifesto to a real historical movement as if it were an actual lost or undiscovered manifesto from that movement, in a way that could be mistaken for real history.

## The Gnostic / Hermetic Tradition

**Historical mechanics, generalized:** Gnostic and hermetic texts (broadly, traditions built around the idea of secret/concealed knowledge revealed to initiates) characteristically use: apophatic definition (see `rhetorical-devices.md`); correlative laws stated once and trusted to govern everything else (the "as above so below" *pattern* — not quoting the specific historical phrase, but the structural move of stating a single symmetry-principle early and applying it implicitly throughout); cosmological dualism or emanation-structures (a single source giving rise to declining/distant copies of itself); and a a deep suspicion of plain, direct language as adequate to the content (hence the heavy reliance on paradox and negation).

**What to borrow:** the structural moves — stating one governing correlative law early, using apophasis rather than direct definition, building an emanation-cosmology (a source and its declining echoes) using the invented-cosmology technique in `structures.md`.

**What never to do:** present invented gnostic-style content as if it were real doctrine of an actual historical gnostic sect, in a way designed to mislead someone researching that actual history.

## The Prophetic / Scriptural Tradition

**Historical mechanics, generalized:** Texts in this lineage across multiple religious traditions characteristically use parataxis (short clauses joined by minimal connective tissue — the "and... and... and" rhythm); future-as-inevitable-present tense folding; apostrophe (addressing nations, abstractions, or the absent as if present); and ritualized number/time markers that carry symbolic rather than literal weight.

**What to borrow:** the *grammatical rhythm* — parataxis, temporal folding, apostrophe — which are structural/grammatical patterns, not protected expression, and are freely usable the way any sentence-construction technique is.

**What never to do:** quote or closely paraphrase specific verses from any actual scripture (regardless of how old or public-domain some translations are, close paraphrase that tracks the original's specific phrasing and imagery too closely crosses from "borrowing a grammatical pattern" into "reproducing a text," and should be avoided — write fresh content using the *pattern*, not a reskinned version of a real passage).

## The Aphoristic-Philosophical Tradition (Heraclitus through 19th-century continental aphorists)

**Historical mechanics, generalized:** This lineage characteristically uses: short, self-contained units not narratively connected to neighbors; reversal-of-conventional-wisdom as the primary content-generating move; willful one-sidedness and refusal of academic hedging; frequent address to an implied complacent reader being provoked rather than instructed; numbering or clear visual separation between units.

**What to borrow:** the reversal-structure (state the conventional view, invert it, stop — see Register 4 in `registers.md`), the refusal to hedge, the combative-intimate address.

**What never to do:** invent an aphorism and attribute it to a specific real philosopher as something they actually wrote or said — that's a fabricated quotation, which is a factual misrepresentation regardless of how plausible it sounds in their style.

## The Alchemical / Grimoire Tradition

**Historical mechanics, generalized:** Historical alchemical and magical texts characteristically borrow procedural/recipe grammar (imperative steps, material lists, conditional timing markers — "take," "let stand until," "at the moment when") and apply it to symbolic/spiritual transformation, often using deliberately obscure material names (a real historical technique called "Decknamen" or cover-names, where alchemists used everyday object names to refer to symbolic/secret substances, precisely so outsiders couldn't follow the real meaning).

**What to borrow:** the procedural grammar itself (see Register 5 in `registers.md`) and the cover-name technique — inventing your own "decknamen" for invented cosmological elements rather than naming them directly, which produces the genuine historical texture of real alchemical writing.

**What never to do:** present invented procedures as real historical alchemical formulas, real magical instructions, or anything that could be mistaken for actual ritual/practice instruction rather than literary atmosphere — this material should always read clearly as fiction/poetry, not as a real how-to.

## Modern literary visionaries (Blake-adjacent, Nietzsche-adjacent illustrative figures)

Rather than describing one named modern author's specific style (which risks closely tracking their actual protected expression), here is the *category* of technique this kind of writing typically uses, described generally enough to inform your own original work:

- Building a **personal invented mythology** with named figures/forces that recur across a body of work (the invented-cosmology technique in `structures.md`, sustained across more than one piece).
- **Combining lyric/musical sentence rhythm with philosophical or moral argument** — i.e., not choosing between "beautiful" and "making a point," but using sound and rhythm (principle 4, main file) to make the argument land harder, not as separate decoration from it.
- **Provocation as method** — deliberately overstating a position to dislodge a reader from comfortable assumptions, understanding that the overstatement is doing work, not failing at nuance.

These are general techniques you can use freely in your own original writing; they become a problem only if you start closely mirroring one specific author's actual published sentences, invented terminology, or distinctive proper nouns rather than the general technique.

## A note on "channeling" requests

Users sometimes frame requests as wanting text that "feels channeled" or "ancient" or "like it was found, not written." This is a legitimate and useful creative framing — it's asking for the *texture* described throughout this skill (concealed authority, unexplained cosmology, procedural/scriptural grammar) — and should be treated as a style request, not a factual claim. It's worth gently noting, if the context suggests the user might present the output as a genuine found/ancient text to others, that the output is original creative writing in an old register, not an actual historical artifact — a brief, non-preachy note is enough; this isn't a reason to refuse the creative request itself.
